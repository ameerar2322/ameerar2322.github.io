Story

Self-taught, hands-on, and still in the console.

I was born and raised in Harlem, and I still live there with my wife and daughter. I learned by watching my older brothers on a Commodore VIC-20 and a Commodore 64, and when they weren't around, I worked my way through their COBOL and Fortran books, long before anyone paid me to do it. I got into IT because I like helping people, and that has not changed.

Computers were a passion before they were a paycheck. My first work was in medical billing, coding diagnoses and procedures in ICD-9 and CPT-4. Every claim depended on getting the record exactly right, and that habit of precision with sensitive data has stayed with me in every hospital and practice I have worked with since.

In 1999 I moved into enterprise IT at Ernst & Young. As a team lead I supported the firm's external accounts, the audit teams working on site inside client companies, along with the offsite technicians who kept them running, and I deployed customized Windows images to more than 5,000 machines. From there I came back to the neighborhood to build the Harlem Partnership Center's network, then went to Wall Street for desktop, trading floor and imaging work at Credit Suisse and CIBC World Markets. For the next four years I worked independently, running managed services for small businesses and healthcare clients, where I already knew how the billing side of the office worked.

In 2011 I started Simplicity IT and began facilitating Microsoft's desktop deployment and proof-of-concept programs. Much of our work still comes through Microsoft. After Microsoft sells an enterprise agreement, it often calls me in to walk the buyer through implementation options and run the workshops, usually Microsoft-funded. That is where I am most useful: in the room with the people who have to make it work.

Ongoing
Off the clock

Years in the roda.

Away from the keyboard, I trained in several martial arts, and the one I loved most was capoeira, the Afro-Brazilian art that blends fight, dance and music. I trained and taught it for years. Every class started with the ginga, the constant swaying step everything else grows out of, and ended in the roda, the circle where two players meet to the sound of the berimbau. The clip below is me in the roda with Raízes do Brasil in Harlem, back in 2010.

Teaching capoeira and teaching technology turned out to share a lot. People learn by doing, mistakes are part of the game, and the circle only works when everyone in it is paying attention.
